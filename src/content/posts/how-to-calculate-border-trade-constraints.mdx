These are my notes on chapter 3 of *The Evolution of Electricity Markets in
Europe* by Leonardo Meeus (2020, written with Tim Schittekatte).
[[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|Chapter 2]] explained how
Europe allocates cross-border capacity through one coupled auction. This
chapter is about the number that auction consumes: **how many MW can safely
cross each border?** Someone has to compute that, every day, for every
border, and the computation is nowhere near as innocent as it sounds,
because the borders are commercial fictions while electricity follows
physics.

## 1. ⚡ Why constraints rule everything

Three facts set the stage. Transmission lines are currently the only
economically viable way to move electricity, and the network is a planned,
regulated monopoly, so it is imperfect by construction. Planning is slow:
wind and solar parks get built faster than the lines to carry them, so the
cheapest generation often cannot reach the willing buyers. And operating
near the limits is dangerous: overload a line and it trips, dumping its flow
onto neighbours, which can cascade. Grids therefore run on the **N−1
principle**: the system must survive the loss of any single element.

<Callout icon="🇮🇹" title="What a cascade looks like: Italy, 28 September 2003">
  At 03:01 a Swiss 380 kV line sagged into a tree and tripped. Italy was
  importing 6,400 MW on its northern border; the Swiss TSO asked for a
  300 MW reduction, which was not enough. At 03:25 a second Swiss line
  tripped, the remaining lines to Italy failed almost simultaneously, and
  twelve seconds later Italy was electrically an island. Frequency fell to
  47.5 Hz and the whole country blacked out within two and a half minutes,
  affecting 56 million people; 30,000 were stranded on trains, and Sicily
  waited until 21:40 for full power. One tree, one country dark: this is why
  border capacity is calculated cautiously, and why the coordination bodies
  in section 4 exist.
</Callout>

## 2. 🗺️ The borders are virtual

Prices in Europe are **zonal**: inside a bidding zone the wholesale price is
one number and trade is unlimited; scarcity of wires only appears *between*
zones. Zones mostly equal countries (Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Italy are
split; Luxembourg shares Germany's zone). Everyone always knew these borders
were virtual: a "border" is just an aggregation of physical lines, and the
binding constraint is often a line deep inside a country.

That makes the classic method, publishing a fixed **net transfer capacity
(NTC)** per border, an almost impossible task. How much a border can take
depends on how flows distribute over many parallel paths, which depends on
production and consumption patterns that are only known *after* trading. And
each border's capacity depends on what happens on every other border. TSOs
guessed conservatively, because they pay penalties when they promise
capacity they cannot deliver.

The successor is **flow-based market coupling (FBMC)**: instead of one
number per border, the TSOs hand the market algorithm the actual constraint
structure. Each TSO nominates its **critical network elements (CNEs)**, the
lines that cross-zonal trade can overload. For each CNE, two parameters go
to EUPHEMIA: **zonal PTDFs** (power transfer distribution factors: how many
MW flow over this line per MW of net export from each zone) and the **RAM**
(remaining available margin: the line's capacity minus the flow already
there from other transactions, minus a reliability margin, minus a
TSO-specific adjustment). The market then clears *inside* the space those
constraints allow. Play with that space:

The two preset corners are the whole argument for FBMC: fixed border values
both waste trade the grid could carry and permit combinations it cannot.
Flow-based went live in Central Western Europe in May 2015, the CACM
guideline made it the EU's primary approach, and after the book it spread:
the Core region (thirteen countries) on 8 June 2022 and the Nordics on
29 October 2024.

## 3. 🌀 The flows that eat borders (annex 3A.2)

Two kinds of flow consume cross-zonal capacity without being the trade the
capacity was meant for. Both exist because electricity spreads over all
parallel paths, but they differ in the one way that matters for market
design: whether the cause is *visible* to the capacity calculation.

<LoopFlowExplorer />

- A **transit flow** comes from a scheduled trade between two *other* zones.
  It is unscheduled from the victim's point of view, but the causing trade
  is cross-zonal, so a coordinated, flow-based calculation can account for
  it and arbitrate: exactly what FBMC does.
- A **loop flow** comes from a transaction *inside* one zone. Within a zone
  trade is unlimited and invisible, so no capacity calculation can price it.
  The book's verdict is blunt: loop flows "can only be addressed by revising
  the bidding zone configuration".

Loop flows are not a footnote; they are the chapter's real open issue. The
worst constraints in today's network are no longer at the borders but inside
the zones, and countries have answered in two opposite ways, each shaped by
a European competition case:

- **The Swedish way: redraw the zones.** Sweden's TSO used to curb exports
  to Denmark to relieve its own internal congestion. After DG COMP found
  this discriminatory (an abuse of dominance favouring internal flows),
  Sweden split into four bidding zones on 1 November 2011. Internal scarcity
  became visible in prices, and cross-border traders compete on equal
  footing.
- **The German way: keep the zone, pay the difference.** Germany keeps one
  zone despite massive north-south congestion, exporting loop flows to its
  neighbours. After its own DG COMP case (opened 2018), the TSO guaranteed
  minimum capacity on the Danish border rather than split the zone. The
  internal congestion is then managed by **redispatch**: paying plants on
  both sides of the bottleneck to deviate. ACER put EU redispatch costs at
  about €2 billion already in 2017, and the book's dry remark stands: those
  who caused the problem get paid to solve it. The one zone change that did
  happen in the region was Germany-Austria: that border got congestion
  management on 1 October 2018.

## 4. 📏 The 70% rule (annex 3A.3, the part with teeth)

The Clean Energy Package's answer to zones that hoard their grid is
Regulation 2019/943, Article 16(8): **at least 70% of the capacity of every
transmission element that limits cross-zonal trade must be made available to
that trade.** The remaining 30% is all a TSO may use for reliability
margins, loop flows and internal flows combined. In flow-domain language:
the rule puts a floor under the RAM you just dragged around. It applies
since January 2020, with full compliance due by 31 December 2025.

<Toggle title="The fine print worth knowing before someone quizzes you">
  **Three escape routes exist.** (a) A Member State with identified
  **structural congestion** (congestion that is unambiguous, predictable,
  geographically stable and frequently recurring, per Article 2) may adopt
  an **action plan** with a linear trajectory reaching 70% by the 2025
  deadline. (b) Regulators may grant **derogations** on foreseeable
  operational security grounds, one year at a time, renewable once, with a
  shrinking scope. (c) As a last resort, regional coordination centres may
  order coordinated capacity reductions for security.

  **And one threat.** A country that neither complies nor adopts an action
  plan, or adopts one and misses its trajectory, can face a **bidding zone
  reconfiguration** through the review process. The rule is, in effect,
  the regulator's answer to the Swedish-or-German choice: keep your single
  zone if you insist, but you no longer get to fund it with your
  neighbours' borders.
</Toggle>

## 5. 🛰️ Who calculates, and with what data

Capacity calculation started as TSO volunteering and hardened into
institutions. Service centres like Coreso (Brussels) and TSCNET (Munich)
appeared in 2008; the System Operation guideline (2017) formalised them as
**regional security coordinators (RSCs)**, one covering every control area;
the Clean Energy Package upgraded them into **regional coordination centres
(RCCs)** with more tasks and stronger governance. For market purposes,
Europe is divided into **capacity calculation regions (CCRs)**; ACER, acting
because regulators could not agree, merged central western and central
eastern Europe into one giant **Core** region. In each CCR the RCC acts as
the coordinated capacity calculator, though TSOs keep final responsibility
for security and may reject an RCC's coordinated action on security grounds
(only; not for economics).

The data pipeline underneath, in one breath: grid users above 220 kV must
provide generation and load data (the GLDPM methodologies); each TSO builds
an **individual grid model (IGM)** of its area per timeframe; the RCCs merge
the IGMs into a **common grid model (CGM)**, the shared forecast of the
whole European system on which capacities are computed; ENTSO-E stores it
all in a common data environment, and a dedicated physical network carries
the exchange. The days when every TSO computed capacity on its own private
grid model are over.

## 6. 🔭 Where this is heading

FBMC solved the border arithmetic but not the geography. The bidding zone
map is the outdated layer: the first EU-wide review (2018) assessed
alternative configurations and recommended the status quo, to most
observers' disappointment, including the authors'. The Clean Energy Package
rebuilt the review process (TSOs analyse configurations with an agreed
methodology; Member States decide; the Commission decides as last resort if
they cannot), and the 70% rule adds the deadline pressure.

The radical endpoint the chapter sketches is the US answer: **nodal
pricing**. Abolish zones, price every node in the grid, and the market
coupling algorithm sees the real network instead of virtual borders. Prices
would form a "weather map" across the continent, changing with wind, sun
and temperature. Studies the book cites put the operating-cost savings of
nodal over zonal for Europe in the order of €0.8-2 billion per year, with
better investment signals but politically sensitive redistribution. Europe
would be the first to try it at continental scale. The
[[gb-market-workshop|GB post]] shows the same debate ending
differently: one national zone, kept in 2025, with constraint costs to
match.

<Toggle title="Acronym cheat sheet for this chapter">
  **NTC**: net transfer capacity, one fixed MW value per border, the old
  method.

  **FBMC**: flow-based market coupling, capacity and flow factors calculated
  jointly and handed to the market algorithm as constraints.

  **CNE**: critical network element, a line (cross-border or internal) that
  cross-zonal trade can overload.

  **PTDF**: power transfer distribution factor, MW of flow on a CNE per MW
  of a zone's net export.

  **RAM**: remaining available margin, what day-ahead trade may still put on
  a CNE after pre-existing flows, a reliability margin (FRM) and a TSO
  adjustment (FAV) are subtracted.

  **GSK**: generation shift key, the assumption translating a zone's net
  position into nodal injections to approximate zonal PTDFs.

  **RSC / RCC**: regional security coordinator, renamed and upgraded to
  regional coordination centre by the Clean Energy Package.

  **CCR**: capacity calculation region; Core is the big merged one.

  **IGM / CGM**: individual and common grid model, the per-TSO forecasts
  and their pan-European merge.

  **GLDPM / KORRR**: the methodologies obliging grid users to provide data
  and organising who exchanges what with whom.

  **N−1**: the security principle that the grid must survive losing any
  single element.
</Toggle>
