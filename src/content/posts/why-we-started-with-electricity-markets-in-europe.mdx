Notes on chapter 1 of Leonardo Meeus, *The Evolution of Electricity Markets in
Europe* (Edward Elgar, 2020), written with Valerie Reif.

The chapter is organised around three questions, and they map onto three
different kinds of answer — political, technical, and economic. Keeping them
separate is most of the value: the reasons Europe *built* a single market are
not the same as the reasons it *works*.

## 1. The political process

Integration happened in three layers, each more detailed than the last: the
treaties set the goal, the legislative packages restructured the industry, and
the network codes wrote the actual market rules.

**The treaties.** The ambition of a common market with no internal trade
barriers goes back to the Treaty of Rome (1957). The Single European Act (1986)
made it concrete by requiring an internal market by 31 December 1992, and the
Single Market became reality on 1 January 1993 for the then-12 Member States.
Energy took much longer than expected, because the sector was still dominated by
state-owned, vertically integrated utilities holding regional or national
monopolies, with little interconnection and no rules for cross-border trade.

Two institutions came out of this: **ENTSO-E** (Third Package) and **ACER**
(Third Package), with an **EU DSO entity** added by the Clean Energy Package.
Ownership unbundling became the dominant model in practice, and national
regulators were pushed step by step towards genuine independence — the Second
Package required them to exist, the Third made them independent of government.

**The network codes.** Eight legislative acts entered into force between 2015 and
the end of 2017, in three families.

<Toggle title="The first generation of network codes and guidelines">
  **Market codes** — CACM GL (capacity allocation and congestion management),
  FCA GL (forward capacity allocation), EB GL (electricity balancing).

  **Connection codes** — RfG NC (requirements for generators), DC NC (demand
  connection), HVDC NC.

  **Operation codes** — SO GL (system operation), ER NC (emergency and
  restoration).

  Four are guidelines and four are network codes. Both are Commission
  regulations and directly applicable, but guidelines push more of the detail
  into the implementation phase, via Terms and Conditions or Methodologies
  (TCMs) developed by TSOs or NEMOs and approved by regulators.
</Toggle>

<Callout icon="🔑" title="The insight I want to remember">
  The rules do not prescribe one market design. Meeus's framing is that they
  "reduce the degree of freedom that countries have" — the design is left
  *implicit*, and making it explicit is the stated purpose of the book. That
  reframes the whole thing: there is no single European market design document
  to go read.
</Callout>

## 2. The technical drivers

The chapter's argument is that there are technical **advantages of scale**, and
that cross-border cooperation on these grounds *pre-dated* markets entirely.

Meeus explains it with a tandem-bicycle analogy: generators and loads are
cyclists on one long bike, the chain is the transmission network, chain speed is
frequency and chain tension is voltage. Different generator types couple to the
chain differently — directly (large thermal and nuclear), through gears (hydro),
or through a belt allowing variable speed (wind with a frequency inverter).

Two mechanisms matter:

- **Inertia.** More synchronously connected rotating machines means more stored
  kinetic energy, which slows frequency deviations after a sudden mismatch.
- **Reserves.** Inertia only buys time; it cannot restore balance. Reserves do
  that, and a bigger synchronous area needs proportionally fewer of them,
  because the largest single credible incident shrinks relative to system size.
  Hence reserve-sharing "solidarity" schemes between TSOs, long before markets.

<Callout icon="⚡" title="Worth being precise about">
  Inertia helps with *near-instantaneous* imbalances — a generator tripping, a
  sudden load change. It does **not** help with ordinary imbalances caused by
  forecast error, where production and consumption plans simply diverge. Those
  need reserves. Easy to blur these two together.
</Callout>

## 3. What we know about the benefits

The qualitative case has been understood for decades and rests on
**differences** between countries:

- **Generation mix** — France nuclear-dominated, Germany still coal-heavy,
  Norway hydro-rich, UK/Spain/Italy relatively gas-heavy.
- **Load patterns** — countries with lots of electric heating (France, Norway)
  swing much harder between summer and winter.
- **Timing** — demand peaks do not coincide across time zones and cultures.
- **Weather** — and more recently the big one: uneven wind and solar across
  Member States.

The quantitative case arrived surprisingly late. The Commission's early-2000s
benchmarking reports tracked indicators — concentration, the share of the three
largest generators and suppliers, switching rates, price convergence,
cross-border exchange — but none of them actually quantified the benefit of
integration. Only around 2012 did the Commission commission that assessment,
which put it at **several billions of euros a year**, an order of magnitude ACER
later confirmed. ACER's annual market monitoring report is the standing
reference.

The Brexit counterfactual is the mirror image: a hard electricity Brexit with
little interconnector expansion and decoupled markets was estimated to cost
Great Britain **several hundred million euros a year by 2030**.
