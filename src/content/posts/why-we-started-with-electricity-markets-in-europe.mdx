These are my notes on chapter 1 of *The Evolution of Electricity Markets in
Europe* by Leonardo Meeus (2020, written with Valerie Reif). Meeus is a
professor at Vlerick Business School and at the Florence School of Regulation,
the institute that trains a lot of Europe's energy regulators. The book's
promise is unusual: the European market design is spread across thousands of
pages of legislation that never state the design explicitly, and the book
reconstructs it.

The chapter asks one question — why did Europe build a single electricity
market? — and gives three answers that are easy to mix up:

1. **A political answer**: the EU wanted a single market for everything, and
   electricity was one of the last holdouts.
2. **A technical answer**: connected power systems are physically easier and
   cheaper to keep stable, which is why grids were interconnected decades
   *before* anyone talked about markets.
3. **An economic answer**: countries have different power plants, different
   weather and different consumption patterns, so trading between them saves
   real money — billions per year.

I go through them in order, with some context the book assumes you already
have.

## 1. 🏛️ The political story: three layers of rules

Before the 1990s, electricity in most of Europe worked like this: each country
had one utility (or a few regional ones), usually owned by the state, that did
everything itself — it owned the power plants, the transmission lines, the
distribution grids, and it sold the electricity to you. The industry term for
this is a **vertically integrated monopoly**: one company controlling every
step of the chain, with no competition at any step. Cables to neighbouring
countries existed but were used sparingly, mostly for emergencies, and there
were no common rules for trading across them.

Turning that into one European market took three layers of rules, each more
detailed than the previous one: treaties, then "packages" of legislation, then
network codes.

### The treaties set the goal

The ambition of a common European market with no internal trade barriers goes
back to the Treaty of Rome (1957), the founding treaty of what became the EU.
The Single European Act (1986) made it concrete: it required an internal market
to be established by 31 December 1992, and on 1 January 1993 the Single Market
became reality for the twelve member states of the time.

Electricity missed that deadline by decades. Goods like cars or food could be
traded once tariffs and standards were harmonised, but electricity had no
markets to open up in the first place — the national monopolies had to be
broken apart before anyone could compete. The year 1993 turned out to be the
starting point, not the finish line.

### The packages restructured the industry

The actual restructuring came through four waves of EU legislation, known as
the energy packages. Two terms help to read the table below:

- A **directive** is an EU law that each country must translate into its own
  national law (with some freedom in how).
- A **regulation** applies directly, word for word, in every member state.

A few of the recurring terms, in plain words:

- **Unbundling** means separating the grid from the rest of the business. The
  logic: the grid is a monopoly (nobody builds a second set of power lines),
  so whoever runs it must not favour their own power plants. It happened in
  steps — first separate *accounting* (1996), then separate *legal entities*
  (2003), then the Third Package required full separation, with **ownership
  unbundling** (the grid company has different owners than the generators)
  becoming the dominant model in Europe.
- A **TSO** (transmission system operator) runs the high-voltage grid of a
  country and is responsible for keeping it stable. **ENTSO-E** is the
  association through which all European TSOs must cooperate — it drafts the
  technical and market rules and coordinates grid planning.
- An **NRA** (national regulatory authority) is a country's energy regulator.
  **ACER** is the EU agency that coordinates the NRAs and decides when they
  cannot agree on cross-border matters.

  speaking its language matters more than you would think.

### The network codes wrote the actual market rules

The packages built the institutions but not the detailed trading rules. Those
came in a third layer: eight EU regulations called **network codes and
guidelines**, all entering into force between 2015 and the end of 2017. They
were drafted mainly by ENTSO-E (the TSOs themselves), reviewed by ACER, and
adopted by the European Commission — the industry proposes, the regulators
challenge.

<Toggle title="The eight network codes and guidelines, by family">
  **Market codes** — how electricity is traded across borders:
  CACM GL (capacity allocation and congestion management — the day-ahead and
  intraday markets), FCA GL (forward markets), EB GL (balancing markets).

  **Connection codes** — what anything plugged into the grid must be able to
  do: RfG NC (generators), DC NC (demand), HVDC NC (high-voltage DC links).

  **Operation codes** — how TSOs run the system: SO GL (system operation),
  ER NC (emergencies and restoration).

  Four are "network codes" and four are "guidelines". The practical
  difference: guidelines leave more detail to be worked out later, through
  methodologies (called TCMs) that TSOs and power exchanges develop and
  regulators approve. That implementation phase is where much of the real
  design work happens — the book notes it was expected to run until around
  2025.
</Toggle>

<Callout icon="🔑" title="The chapter's key idea">
  Even with thousands of pages of rules, there is no document that says "this
  is the European market design". Meeus's framing: the rules only "reduce the
  degree of freedom that countries have". The design is implicit, and making
  it explicit is the purpose of the book. That explains something I had
  noticed before: you cannot go read the European market design anywhere —
  you have to reconstruct it.
</Callout>

## 2. ⚙️ The technical story: one big machine

This part needs one piece of background. Almost all of the European
continent's grid is **synchronous**: every conventional generator from
Portugal to (since 2025) the Baltic states spins in lockstep, producing
alternating current at the same 50 Hz rhythm. Physically it behaves as one
machine. This Continental Europe synchronous area is the largest synchronous
grid in the world, serving over 400 million people — and it was built up
decade by decade *before* electricity markets existed, for engineering
reasons, not commercial ones.

The book explains those reasons with a tandem-bicycle analogy: generators and
consumers are cyclists on one very long bicycle, the chain is the transmission
grid, the chain's speed is the frequency and its tension is the voltage. Some
cyclists pedal directly on the chain (large thermal and nuclear plants), some
through gears (hydro), some through a belt that lets them pedal at their own
pace (wind turbines behind an inverter). The system works only if the chain
keeps constant speed and tension, whatever any individual cyclist does.

Two mechanisms make a *bigger* bicycle better:

- **Inertia.** Every spinning generator stores kinetic energy, like a
  flywheel. When a big power plant suddenly fails, that stored energy is
  released automatically and slows down the frequency drop, buying the
  operators seconds to react. More connected machines means more inertia, so
  the same incident causes a smaller disturbance.
- **Reserves.** Inertia only buys time — it cannot restore the balance
  between production and consumption. That is done by reserves: plants
  deliberately held back from full output so they can jump in. A stand-alone
  country must keep enough reserves to survive losing its single largest
  plant. In a shared system that worst case barely grows, while the pool of
  helpers grows enormously — so everyone can hold proportionally fewer
  reserves. TSOs set up exactly such "solidarity" reserve-sharing schemes
  long before markets existed.

There is a modern illustration of both mechanisms. On 8 January 2021 the
Continental European grid **split in two** after an overloaded substation
tripped in Croatia and the failure cascaded. The north-west part suddenly
lacked 6.3 GW; frequency fell to 49.74 Hz within 15 seconds. It was contained
because the system responded as designed: about 1.7 GW of industrial customers
in France and Italy that are paid to be disconnectable were switched off, and
neighbouring grids (the Nordic area and Great Britain) pushed in support over
their DC links. The two halves were resynchronised about an hour later. In a
small isolated system, the same event would have meant blackouts.

<Callout icon="⚡" title="Worth being precise about">
  Inertia helps only with <em>near-instantaneous</em> imbalances — a plant
  tripping, a sudden change in load. It does <strong>not</strong> help with
  ordinary imbalances caused by forecast errors, where production and
  consumption plans simply drift apart. Those are handled by reserves and the
  balancing market. This distinction is directly relevant to forecasting
  work: the imbalance a better forecast removes is precisely the part that
  inertia never covered.
</Callout>

## 3. 💶 The economic story: differences are worth billions

Trade is valuable when the trading partners are different, and European
power systems are *very* different. The book lists the differences
qualitatively; here is what they look like in 2024 data:

France runs on nuclear, Norway on hydro, Italy mostly on gas, Germany and
Spain increasingly on wind and solar. On top of the generation mix, demand
differs too: countries with lots of electric heating (France, Norway) swing
much harder between summer and winter, daily peaks fall at different hours
across time zones and cultures, and — increasingly the dominant factor — wind
and sun do not blow and shine in the same places at the same time. Every one
of these differences is an opportunity: when French nuclear output is cheap at
night it can displace German gas; when it is windy in the North Sea, exports
flow south.

### How big are the gains? The number arrived surprisingly late

The qualitative argument was understood for decades — even before
liberalisation, utilities sometimes preferred a long-term import contract with
a neighbour over building a new domestic plant. But nobody put a number on
integration for a long time. The European Commission's benchmarking reports of
the early 2000s tracked indicators (market concentration, switching rates,
price convergence, use of interconnectors) without ever quantifying what
integration was worth.

The first real quantification came only in 2013, when the Commission had
consultants (Booz & Company, with economists David Newbery and Goran Strbac)
assess it. Their headline results:

- Coupling the day-ahead markets alone — making sure cheap electricity
  automatically flows to where prices are high — is worth **€2.5–4 billion
  per year**, roughly €5–8 per European per year. About 58–66% of that was
  already being captured in 2013.
- Full market integration was estimated at **€12.5–40 billion per year by
  2030**, and coordinating where renewables get built across borders could
  add another €16–30 billion per year on top.

ACER, the EU regulators' agency, later confirmed the order of magnitude and
now tracks it continuously in its annual Market Monitoring Report. Its most
recent headline estimate is that cross-border trade delivered about **€34
billion of benefits in 2021**.

### The natural experiment: Brexit

Brexit turned the question around: what does it cost to *leave*? Researchers
at Imperial College and Cambridge modelled a "hard Elecxit" — Great Britain
decoupled from the EU market with little further interconnector expansion —
and estimated it would raise generation costs by roughly **€560–700 million
per year by 2030** (about 1.5–2%), with Great Britain bearing around 60% of
the loss.

This stopped being hypothetical after the book was published. On 1 January
2021 Great Britain did leave the internal energy market. Electricity still
flows over the cables, but trading reverted to the clumsy old method the EU
had spent years eliminating: traders must buy capacity on each cable in a
separate auction, then trade the energy separately — so power sometimes flows
from the expensive side to the cheap side, which coupled markets make
impossible. The EU–UK trade agreement sketched a replacement mechanism
("multi-region loose volume coupling"), but five years on it still has not
been implemented; as of late 2025 the TSOs' technical reports were still
under discussion, and the industry on both sides was openly calling for a
return to proper price coupling.
