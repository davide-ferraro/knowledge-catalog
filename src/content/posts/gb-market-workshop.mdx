Two half-hours, eighteen months apart. On the evening of 8 January 2025 the
GB imbalance price hit **£2,900 per megawatt-hour**. On a sunny bank holiday
afternoon, 6 April 2026, it settled at **minus £97.92**: generators paid to
produce. Both numbers came out of exactly the same calculation, and that
calculation, called **cash-out**, is what this post builds from first
principles. It is one number per half-hour, and every market party that was
long or short against its contracted position settles at it.

from the rest of Europe. The design in force today dates from 2001; what

## 2. ⏱️ The trading day: 48 half-hours, one hard stop

First the physical setting: GB has well over 100 GW of nameplate capacity
(gas 36.5 GW, solar 22.3, offshore wind 16.7, onshore wind 16.6,
interconnectors 10.3, batteries 7.2, nuclear 5.9, plus bioenergy and hydro)
against an average demand of only 30 to 38 GW depending on season. That gap
is the whole story of any given day: which sliver of the fleet actually
runs, and at what price.

The day itself is a sequence with one hard stop:

1. **Day-ahead auctions.** Three every day: EPEX SPOT clears hourly at
   09:20 and half-hourly at 15:30, N2EX (Nord Pool) hourly at 09:50. GB
   left EU coupling at the end of 2020, so the two exchanges clear apart
   and can diverge. These auctions are voluntary venues, not the mechanism
   the market must pass through, but they are institutionally load-bearing:
   the hourly results of both are volume-weighted into the **Intermittent
   Market Reference Price** that every wind and solar CfD settles against
   (baseload CfDs settle against a seasonal forward price instead; the
   note after this list unpacks both). That is why wind sells day-ahead at
   the forecast, and why the forecast *error* is what gets traded
   afterwards.
2. **Intraday.** Two more auctions (17:30 on D-1 and 08:00 on the day,
   coupled with the Irish market over the interconnectors), then continuous
   trading in half-hour, hour, 2h and 4h blocks until close to real time.
   Positions adjust as forecasts move.
3. **Gate closure, one hour before delivery.** Every unit's physical plan
   (the **FPN**, final physical notification) and every party's contract
   positions freeze. Whatever is still wrong after this point becomes
   imbalance.
4. **Delivery.** NESO balances the system with **Bids and Offers** from BM
   units, plus trades outside the mechanism. GB is **self-dispatch**:
   nobody is told what to generate; every plant runs its own plan and NESO
   buys deviations from it.
5. **Cash-out.** An indicative imbalance price is published about 15
   minutes after each half-hour. Single price for both directions since
   2015: metered minus contracted volume, per party, and you settle the
   gap at the system price whether you are long or short.

<Callout icon="🧮" title="Volume-weighted, and who gets which reference price">
  **Volume-weighted** means the price where more energy actually traded
  counts for more. For a given hour, EPEX and N2EX each clear their own
  auction, so there are two prices and two traded volumes. Say EPEX clears
  at **£52/MWh** with **8,000 MWh** traded and N2EX clears at **£50/MWh**
  with **2,000 MWh**. The reference price for that hour is not the simple
  average of £51:

  <p class="text-center font-mono">(£52 × 8,000 + £50 × 2,000) ÷ 10,000 = **£51.60/MWh**</p>

  The busier exchange pulls the number towards its price. That is the
  point: a reference built this way is hard to nudge by trading a small
  volume on the thinner venue.

  Which reference price a CfD settles against is written into the contract
  by **technology class**; the generator does not choose. Weather-driven
  technologies (offshore wind, onshore wind, solar, tidal, wave) are
  classed as **intermittent** and get the hourly IMRP above, because a
  plant that cannot pick when it runs should be measured against the price
  in the actual hours it generated. Technologies that can run around the
  clock (nuclear, so Hinkley Point C, biomass conversions, energy from
  waste) are classed as **baseload** and get the **Baseload Market
  Reference Price**: a volume-weighted average of season-ahead forward
  trades reported by the London brokers, fixed once for each six-month
  season. The logic is symmetric: a dispatchable plant is expected to sell
  its output forward like any other baseload plant, so its CfD tops up the
  forward price it could have locked in, not the day-ahead price on any
  particular day.
</Callout>

Two GB peculiarities to hold onto before the next section. There are **no
zonal prices**, even though the Scotland to England boundary is congested
most windy days; the constraint shows up *inside* the balancing stack
instead, which is section 4. And when the balancing stack cannot produce a
price at all, cash-out falls back to Elexon's **Market Index Price**, a
volume-weighted price of exchange trades in the window before gate closure
(zeroed when volume is below a liquidity threshold).

On a windy day the wind error is the whole story, and *where* it sits
matters as much as its size (section 5). On a tight day a single trip is
the margin, visible first as an availability redeclaration and then as a
REMIT message. On a quiet day everything ties at rank 1 because the system
is finely balanced: 20 MW can be the difference between an on-peak and an
off-peak price.

## 3. 🧮 The stack and the price

Here is the machine. For each half-hour, Elexon takes every balancing
action NESO accepted and turns the whole pile into one number, following
the recipe in BSC Section T. Before the steps, be clear about the problem
the recipe solves: **the raw pile is polluted**. It mixes true energy
actions with things that say nothing about the energy balance. Turning a
wind farm down behind a full cable is a network problem, not a shortage.
Reserve that was booked in advance looks artificially cheap at its
utilisation price. Tiny volumes are noise, and sometimes NESO ends up both
buying and selling the same megawatt-hour. If all of that priced the
half-hour, cash-out would be meaningless. So the recipe cleans the pile
step by step, and only what survives sets the price.

The workshop walked this on a small made-up stack where every action stays
visible: eight Offers, three Bids, system short. Step through it. Each
step's bar chart shows everything the pipeline has done so far:

<PricePipeline />

Two things to take from the walk. First, the price is not "what NESO
paid": most of what NESO paid is flagged away, netted off, or tagged out,
and one surviving megawatt-hour speaks for the whole half-hour. Second,
every strange GB price is just one of these steps doing its job, and now
you can name which one.

The same recipe, run on five real half-hours; the £2,900 and the minus
£97.92 are both in here:

<BalancingStack />

Three lessons the stacks teach:

- **The price is the marginal megawatt-hour, and the ladder is thin.** On
  8 January 2025, Offers up to £5,500 were accepted earlier that evening
  and NIV-tagged out of the settled period; the scarcity price peaked at
  £859 in the neighbouring half-hour and never bound. GB spikes do not come
  from an administered scarcity formula; they come from exhausted ladders
  of unflagged Offers. In three of the four days in the next interactive,
  the action *behind* the marginal one was £100 to £190 away.
- **Batteries set ordinary prices; gas sets spikes.** Between 2023 and 2025
  batteries went from setting the marginal Bid 0.8% of the time to 36%,
  and the marginal Offer from 3% to 27%, displacing gas and pumped
  storage. In the most expensive 5% of periods, though, batteries are
  still under-represented: the tail still belongs to thermal plant.
- **A start is a fixed charge spread over the run.** A gas unit prices an
  Offer as total cost at output plus start-up cost divided across the MW
  and the hours it expects to run, so a three-hour run prices very
  differently from a twelve-hour one. Ramp rates and minimum on and off
  times (Grid Code dynamic data) shape what must be traded around it.

## 4. 🍃 Wind bids at minus its subsidy

To understand the negative end of the stack you need one fact about GB
renewables: **a curtailed wind farm loses its subsidy income**, so the
price at which it is willing to be turned down is minus that subsidy.

- Under the legacy **Renewables Obligation**, an offshore farm earning 2.0
  certificates per MWh gives up roughly £139 for every curtailed MWh, so
  it bids near **minus £139**. Onshore at 0.9 certificates, near minus
  £62. This is why deeply negative Bid ladders exist at all.
- Under a **Contract for Difference**, the floor is minus the difference
  payment (strike minus reference price), because the top-up is paid on
  metered volume only. The same contract seen from the revenue side:

<CfdPayoff />

This floor is about to move. The RO scheme starts expiring: around 5 GW of
supported capacity leaves in 2027 and roughly 25 GW by 2037, taking the
minus £139 bids with it. A live rule change (BSC modification **P462**)
would instead compensate lost subsidy through settlement, so curtailed wind
would bid near zero. Anyone modelling GB negative prices is modelling two
regimes with a boundary in between.

## 5. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿 Curtailment inside the price

GB's one price zone means congestion is invisible in the wholesale price.
It is not invisible in the balancing stack; it is most of it.

When NESO curtails Scottish wind because the north-south boundary (**B6**)
is full, those Bids are **SO-flagged**: taken for the network, not for
energy. Classification then strips a flagged Bid only if it is more
negative than the most expensive unflagged Bid; cheaper than that, it stays
in. The workshop's numbers for September 2025: **80% of all sell-action
volume was SO-flagged**, 74% of it wind, at an average of minus £40. On the
windiest day of the month it was 99%.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **The floor around minus £98 is a market outcome, not a rule.** It is
  the most expensive unflagged Bid, typically an RO wind farm bidding
  minus its subsidy somewhere *south* of the constraint, surviving
  Classification. It breaks only when the system is so long that
  interconnector export trades priced below it get reached.
- **Geography decides which stack prices the period.** The same gigawatt
  of wind forecast error means opposite things on opposite sides of B6:
  north of it, the error lands in flagged Bids that leave the price; south
  of it, it sets cash-out. On 24 January 2025, the night before Storm
  Éowyn, half the Bid volume was flagged away and the *English* stack
  priced the period at exactly £0. A national wind number has the sign
  wrong on the days that matter.

The bill for handling congestion this way: **£1.7bn of thermal constraint
costs in 2024/25**, out of £2.7bn of total balancing costs, on 13.5 TWh of
constraint volume, 81% more volume than the year before. On
top of it, about £99m a year of "repetitive re-trading": storage sells,
gets bid down behind the constraint, sells again (a "storage goes last"
consultation ran in autumn 2026 to close the loop). None of this is in the
wholesale price; all of it ends up in network charges.

<Callout icon="⚖️" title="The rules drawn around the stack">
  Bidding behind a constraint is regulated. The <strong>Transmission
  Constraint Licence Condition</strong> forbids obtaining an excessive
  benefit from being constrained down: CfD wind that kept the avoided
  repayment to the CfD counterparty in its Bids has paid for it
  (Dorenell £5.53m, Beatrice £33.14m in redress, both concluded 2024). The
  <strong>Inflexible Offers Licence Condition</strong> (since October 2023)
  polices plant that de-syncs late and re-offers expensively; per the
  workshop, Ofgem's 2025 review found it effective for exactly that, and
  found the expensive units of 8 January 2025 sat outside it, because they
  had entered the day with no generation plan at all. And REMIT
  applies to everyone: deliberate imbalance positions are lawful for
  non-physical traders, but inside information stays inside.
</Callout>

## 6. 📅 Four real half-hours

Everything so far, as a game a forecaster cannot refuse. First, the
baselines the workshop put on the table, so you know what "good" means:
between January 2025 and September 2026 the *sign* of NIV persisted from
one period to the next 76% of the time (68% two periods ahead), the mean
absolute NIV was only 230 MWh, and 89% of periods stayed under 500 MWh.
Direction is forecastable to roughly 75 to 85% an hour ahead; magnitude is
the hard part, heavy-tailed and set by a 1 MWh sliver after all the
flagging.

Now the game. Four real days, one marked half-hour each. You see the NIV,
the market index and NESO's published forecasts at gate closure; the
settled prices stay hidden until you commit.

<CallThePrice />

And one more half-hour, told in the workshop as a war story: on 4 March
2020 a day-shift trader ran a 300 MW speculative short into the evening
peak, expecting cash-out well below the price already sold. Instead the
scarcity price bound: loss-of-load probability had been 0.006 twelve hours
out and reached 0.37 an hour out, and settlement period 37 settled at
**£2,242.31**, the only price above £150 in the whole preceding winter.
Roughly a £250,000 half-hour. The moral is not "never short the peak"; it
is that the tail of this price is made of plant trips, loss-of-load
probability and reserve pricing, which are system-state inputs, not weather
inputs. On 8 January 2025 the same kicker reached £499 and did not bind:
both behaviours belong in any honest model.

## 7. 🎛️ How NESO dispatches now

The control room is mid-upgrade, which is why models trained on old
balancing data mislead.

- **Open Balancing Platform** (December 2023): bulk dispatch of batteries
  and small units. Battery balancing volumes rose 284% in 2024/25, and
  battery "skip rates" (being passed over despite price) fell from 43% to
  32% during 2025. The remaining skips are mostly constraint and
  system-need driven, not price driven.
- **National Dispatch Optimiser**: parallel running from January 2026,
  business go-live June 2026, with optimisation inside constraints
  following. Dispatch becomes an optimisation, not a merit-order walk.
- **GC0166** (by late 2026): batteries stop being dispatched on a flat
  30-minute assumption; dynamic parameters give NESO a standardised view
  of state of charge for the first time.
- On the horizon: Ofgem decisions on **balancing reform** (5 or 15-minute
  settlement periods, aligning FPNs with traded positions) expected around
  the end of 2027, and market-wide half-hourly settlement of all consumers
  completing in May 2027. The REMA review already settled the biggest
  question in 2025: no zonal prices, so everything in this post stays a
  balancing-stack story.

The practical difference for anyone who forecasts: in the Nordics you trade
the *direction of balancing*, and weather is the input. In GB, NESO's own
wind and demand forecasts are free, public, and already priced into the
09:50 auction, because NESO's errors drive the very actions that set NIV. A
forecast only earns money where it disagrees with NESO and NESO turns out
to be wrong, and *where* the error sits (north or south of B6) can matter
more than its size. The irony from the [[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|chapter 2 notes]]
still applies: the Nordic design is exactly the market splitting GB
rejected in the REMA review, running inside the coupled market GB left.

## 9. 🔌 The borders, in one paragraph each

GB's nine interconnectors (9.2 GW) each need their own allocation
arrangement since Brexit. The default is **explicit auctions**, capacity
sold separately from energy, with all the wrong-way-flow pathologies the
[[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|chapter 2 notes]] documented.
Two exceptions: North Sea Link to Norway runs an **implicit day-ahead
auction** (N2EX and the Norwegian NO2 auction clear together, essentially
the [[third-time-lucky-on-the-kontek-cable|first Kontek design]] done
properly, continuing past 2026 under Nord Pool with other exchanges gaining
access on the Norwegian side), and capacity to Ireland is allocated
implicitly through the two coupled **intraday auctions**. The Brexit
agreement's promised replacement, multi-region loose volume coupling,
remains unimplemented.

## 10. 📡 Why GB is a forecaster's market at all

GB publishes what most markets keep opaque: per BM unit, you can read the
declared plan for every half-hour, its Bids and Offers, what NESO accepted
and with which flags, and the resulting system price calculation, all on
Elexon's Insights platform, with NESO's own forecasts and constraint data
alongside. Everything in this post is reconstructable from public data,
which is also why the GB analytics ecosystem (Modo Energy, EnAppSys, LCP
Delta and friends) is so crowded and so quantitative: everyone can measure
everyone, so new forecasts get benchmarked, not believed.
