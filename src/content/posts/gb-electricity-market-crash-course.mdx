A deliberately short primer. The one thing to hold onto: **Great Britain
invented electricity liberalisation, then chose a different market design
than the rest of Europe**, and most GB-specific jargon follows from that
choice. Everything here is verified and linked; ask me to go deeper on any
section.

## How a MWh gets traded (and why it's not like the EU)

The [[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|European coupled market]]
runs on one big day-ahead auction. GB works differently:

1. **Trade whenever you like.** Generators and suppliers trade bilaterally,
   over the counter and on exchanges, from years ahead to shortly before
   delivery. Day-ahead auctions are part of this landscape (see the box
   below), but as venues you may use, not as a mechanism you must pass
   through.
2. **Notify your position.** For every half-hour settlement period, parties
   tell the system operator what they plan to produce or consume, up to
   **gate closure**, one hour before delivery.
3. **NESO balances the rest** in the **Balancing Mechanism**: generators and
   flexible consumers submit bids and offers to deviate from their plans,
   and NESO accepts them in real time to keep the system stable. Congestion
   is handled here too, as paid redispatch: a constrained-off wind farm is
   paid its accepted bid to turn down, a plant behind the bottleneck is paid
   its offer to turn up.
4. **Elexon settles the differences.** Whoever was out of balance pays or
   receives the **cash-out price** (one single imbalance price per
   half-hour, set by the marginal balancing actions NESO took) for the gap
   between contracts and reality, under the Balancing and Settlement Code.
   Note what this implies: cash-out is built from NESO's actions, so NESO's
   own demand and wind forecast errors move the price everyone settles
   against. And because every position is ultimately priced against expected
   cash-out, those same published forecasts echo backwards into intraday and
   even day-ahead prices. Traders live on forecast revisions: wind, solar,
   demand, outages, gas prices and interconnector flows between the
   day-ahead auction and gate closure.

<Callout icon="❓" title="So does GB have a day-ahead auction or not?">
  It has <strong>three, every day</strong>: EPEX SPOT runs an hourly auction
  at 09:20 and a half-hourly one at 15:30, and Nord Pool's N2EX runs its
  hourly auction at 09:50. They are popular and liquid, a lot of GB power
  changes hands in them, and they are institutionally important: the hourly
  results of the EPEX and N2EX auctions are volume-weighted into the
  official reference price that every wind and solar CfD settles against.
  What GB does <em>not</em> have is the EU arrangement, where the day-ahead
  auction is the backbone of the whole market: one auction, clearing every
  zone at once, allocating the cross-border cables, unavoidable in practice.
  In GB the auctions are ordinary commercial products offered by two
  competing exchanges (two of them partly because they used to feed two
  different EU coupling projects before Brexit). You could trade a GB
  portfolio for years without ever bidding into an auction; in the coupled
  EU market, the auction is where the market happens.
</Callout>

### One worked half-hour

Numbers make the two mechanisms concrete (simplified, but the logic is
exact):

**Cash-out.** In some settlement period the market as a whole is 300 MWh
short: plans said more generation than actually showed up. NESO accepts
offers to fill the gap: 200 MWh at £80/MWh, then 100 MWh at £120/MWh. The
cash-out price is set by the *most expensive* action needed, so it lands at
**£120/MWh** (formally it is the priciest 1 MWh of actions). Now every
party's own gap is settled at that single price: a supplier who was 10 MWh
short pays 10 × £120 = £1,200; a wind farm that was 10 MWh *long* helped the
system and receives £1,200. One price, both directions, which is why
forecasting your own imbalance, and the system's, is worth real money.

**A constraint.** A Scottish cable is full. NESO accepts a wind farm's bid
to curtail 100 MWh at £70/MWh (wind bids are expensive to accept because a
curtailed wind farm loses its subsidy income) and a gas plant's offer south
of the bottleneck to produce 100 MWh at £95/MWh. That one action pair costs
£7,000 + £9,500 = **£16,500 for a single half-hour**, and the wholesale
price shows none of it. Scale that up and you get GB's constraint bill:
**£1.9 billion in 2024/25**, 71% of all balancing costs, heading for a
projected £4-8 billion a year by 2030 without major fixes.

Two structural consequences of the design: GB is **self-dispatch** (every
plant decides its own schedule from its trades, there is no central
optimiser like EUPHEMIA), and GB is **one single price zone**, so the
wholesale price never tells you *where* power is scarce, as the constraint
example just showed.

<Callout icon="⚡" title="Why the zonal pricing fight mattered">
  For three years REMA (the government's market design review) debated
  splitting GB into price zones so that prices would reflect grid congestion,
  Nordic-style. In July 2025 the government said no: one national price
  stays, with "reformed national pricing" (better locational signals in
  network charges and grid connections) instead. Your expert will have an
  opinion on this. Everyone does.
</Callout>

The irony worth savouring: the Nordic design *is* market splitting, the thing
GB decided against in the REMA review, and the Nordics run it inside the
coupled European market that GB left.

### Same roles, different names

The Nordic role vocabulary (BRP, BSP) mostly has GB equivalents; the
functions exist everywhere, the labels don't travel:

- **BRP (balance responsible party).** In the Nordics, a distinct role:
  every consumer and producer must have a BRP that answers financially for
  imbalances, settled centrally by eSett. GB has no separate BRP layer:
  every trading party under the Balancing and Settlement Code simply
  carries its own imbalance exposure, settled by Elexon. Same function,
  folded into the trading role itself.
- **BSP (balancing service provider).** The Nordic term for whoever sells
  balancing energy (mFRR, aFRR) to the TSOs. The GB equivalent is a
  participant in the Balancing Mechanism: any BM Unit can submit bids and
  offers, and since December 2019 independent aggregators can join as
  **Virtual Lead Parties** without being suppliers. Frequency-response
  products (Dynamic Containment and friends) are procured by NESO in
  separate auctions, much like Nordic reserve markets.
- **Imbalance settlement body**: eSett (jointly owned by the Nordic TSOs)
  maps to Elexon.
- **Imbalance pricing**: both ended up in the same place, a single price
  for both directions per settlement period; GB got there in 2015, the
  Nordics on 1 November 2021. The remaining gap is granularity: 30-minute
  periods in GB versus 15-minute in the Nordics.
- **IPP (independent power producer)** is informal industry language in
  both markets, not a defined role: a generator that isn't part of a big
  incumbent utility.

## Where the money actually comes from

The wholesale market is only part of a GB generator's income. The state
instruments on top:

- **Contracts for Difference (CfD)**: the main tool for new low-carbon
  plants. Auctioned "strike price" per MWh; the generator sells in the
  market and the contract tops up (or claws back) the difference. With a
  strike of £75: if the day-ahead reference price is £60, the generator
  gets a £15/MWh top-up; if the reference is £90, it pays £15/MWh back.
  Either way it earns £75, which is the point: price risk gone, financing
  cheap. The latest auction (AR7, results January and February 2026)
  contracted a record 14.7 GW, including 8.4 GW of offshore wind.
- **Capacity Market**: yearly auctions paying plants and flexible demand
  just for being available during scarcity. Born from the fear that an
  energy-only market retires too much firm capacity.
- **Balancing and constraint payments** from NESO, increasingly important
  income for batteries and flexible plants.
- All of it in service of **Clean Power 2030**: at least 95% of generation
  from clean sources by 2030 (2024 actual mix: gas 30%, wind 29%, nuclear
  14%, solar 5%).

## Who actually buys forecasts here

Three kinds of buyers, with different needs:

- **Trading desks** at utilities, funds and independents, positioning across
  day-ahead, intraday and cash-out. They want the forecast *revisions* and
  the system-imbalance view, fast.
- **Battery optimisers and route-to-market providers** (Octopus's Kraken
  platform and dedicated optimisers like Flexitricity are the classic
  names). GB has one of the world's biggest grid battery fleets, and its
  revenue stack (wholesale arbitrage, Balancing Mechanism, frequency
  response) is decided by short-horizon price and dispatch forecasts.
- **Asset owners and operators**: wind and solar portfolios managing
  imbalance exposure and CfD reference-price risk.

A cultural note that follows from the open data below: GB buyers are
unusually quantitative. Because everyone can measure everyone, a new
forecast provider gets benchmarked in a trial against incumbents rather
than bought on a story.

## The data angle: GB shows you everything

One GB trait matters enormously for anyone in the forecasting business: **GB
publishes physical and market data at a depth most European markets keep
opaque**. Elexon's Insights platform exposes, per individual plant (per "BM
Unit"): its declared production plan for every half-hour (the physical
notification), its bids and offers into the Balancing Mechanism, what NESO
accepted, and the resulting detailed system prices. NESO's data portal adds
the system operator's own demand and wind forecasts and constraint-cost
data. In the Nordics you mostly see zone-level aggregates; in GB you can
watch a single battery's behaviour half-hour by half-hour.

That transparency is why GB has a crowded ecosystem of market analytics
vendors (Modo Energy, EnAppSys, LCP Delta and others) and highly quantified
trading desks and battery optimisers as their customers.

## Where the design is heading

Three live reform tracks, each with a forecasting angle:

- **Reformed national pricing** (the REMA outcome): no zonal prices, but
  stronger locational signals through network charges and connections.
  Constraint costs stay a Balancing Mechanism story, which keeps BM
  forecasting valuable.
- **Market-wide half-hourly settlement (MHHS)**: today only large sites
  (above 100 kW) are settled on actual half-hourly data; every other
  consumer is settled on standard profiles. MHHS moves *all* consumers onto
  real smart-meter data, with supplier migration finishing by May 2027. For
  demand forecasting this is a step change: actual half-hourly behaviour of
  the whole retail market, and supplier incentives to shift it.
- **The clean power buildout** itself: every AR7 wind farm that connects
  makes prices more weather-driven, and both balancing and hedging harder.

## Questions worth asking tomorrow

1. What will "reformed national pricing" actually change in practice, and
   does it do anything real about constraint costs?
2. AR7 contracted a record pipeline. Where is the delivery risk: grid
   connections, supply chains, or the CfD terms themselves?
3. Is the Balancing Mechanism genuinely open to batteries and aggregators
   now, and how are imbalance prices behaving as wind share grows?
4. Post-Brexit interconnector trading is still running on the old separate
   auctions. Does anyone in GB seriously expect recoupling with the EU
   market, and who would gain?
5. NESO is one year old as a public body. Has anything actually changed in
   how the system is planned and balanced?
6. Where do GB traders feel underserved on data and forecasts today: shaping
   day-ahead positions, trading intraday, or anticipating the Balancing
   Mechanism and cash-out prices?
7. Who are the established forecast and analytics vendors serving GB traders
   (Modo Energy, EnAppSys, LCP Delta...), why are they popular, and what do
   they consistently get wrong or not cover? How does a GB desk actually
   trial and evaluate a new provider?
8. How exactly do intraday prices and the cash-out price form, and how much
   of cash-out is really predictable from NESO's published forecasts and the
   Balancing Mechanism data?
