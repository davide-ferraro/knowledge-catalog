These are my notes on chapter 2 of *The Evolution of Electricity Markets in
Europe* by Leonardo Meeus (2020, written with Tim Schittekatte). Chapter 1
explained [[why-we-started-with-electricity-markets-in-europe|why Europe built
a single power market]]; this chapter is about the machinery that actually
makes cross-border trade happen.

One idea holds the whole chapter together. **A cable between two countries has
limited capacity, and whoever holds the right to use it holds real economic
power.** If electricity costs €30/MWh in country A and €50/MWh in country B,
moving 1 MWh across earns €20, but only for whoever has the right to use the
cable. The chapter is the story of how Europe took those rights away from the
old monopolies and then spent twenty years figuring out how to hand them out
fairly, separately for each trading timeframe: months and years ahead, the
day before delivery, and the final hours.

## 1. ⚖️ The starting point: rights inherited from the monopoly era

Before liberalisation, the rights to use cross-border cables mostly sat inside
long-term contracts between neighbouring national utilities. When markets
opened in the late 1990s, those contracts hadn't expired, so newcomers who
wanted to import cheap power found the border already "full", legally if not
physically. The First Directive said network access must be
non-discriminatory, but the old privileges survived it.

What broke the deadlock was a court case. In the Netherlands, the regulator
had reserved a large share of the country's import capacity (1,500 of the
3,200 MW available in 2000) for the long-term contracts of SEP, the former
national generation monopoly. Three parties challenged the decision: VEMW (an
association of large industrial energy consumers), the Amsterdam Power
Exchange, and the utility Eneco. The case went up to the European Court of
Justice, which ruled in June 2005 that the reservation was discriminatory: it
protected the incumbent's position and blocked market access for new players,
and the fact that SEP had signed those contracts back when it legally held the
import monopoly did not justify it. The Dutch decision was annulled, and
regulators in other countries used the ruling to dismantle similar privileges.

That created the next problem: how do you allocate the freed-up capacity
fairly? A 2004 survey of European borders shows a patchwork of methods, some
of them barely disguised versions of the old order:

- **Priority lists**: whoever signed up first got the capacity. In practice:
  incumbents and large industrials.
- **Pro-rata rationing**: everyone gets a share proportional to what they
  asked for. In practice: asking for more gets you more, so everyone inflates
  requests.
- **Explicit auctions** and **market splitting**: the first genuinely
  market-based methods, and the subject of the rest of the chapter.

## 2. 🎫 Explicit auctions: sell the cable separately (and watch it fail)

Regulation 1228/2003 (Second Package) made market-based allocation mandatory
but did not pick a method. Most borders started with the approach that
required the least change: **explicit auctions**. The TSOs auction the
transmission capacity itself, as its own product, separately from any
electricity trade. You buy "100 MW of capacity from Germany to the
Netherlands for tomorrow", and separately you buy the energy in Germany and
sell it in the Netherlands.

The flaw is baked into the word *separately*. To know what a capacity right
is worth, you must predict tomorrow's *price difference* between the two
countries, hour by hour, and traders turned out to be bad at that. The
Commission's 2007 Sector Inquiry found that on the German–Dutch border alone,
the value lost to bad guesses in 2004 was about **€50 million, half of the
total value of the trade**. In roughly 40% of the hours, power flowed towards
the Netherlands while Germany was the more expensive side: traders had paid
for capacity and then lost money using it in the wrong direction.

The fix Europe converged on is the **implicit auction**: stop selling
capacity to traders altogether. Give it to the power exchanges instead, and
let their day-ahead auction use it. The algorithm sees the actual bids on
both sides of the border, so it never guesses wrong and never wastes a
profitable megawatt. Because this welds the national day-ahead auctions
together, the approach was renamed **market coupling**.

<Callout icon="🔑" title="The distinction everything else builds on">
  <strong>Explicit</strong>: capacity is a product you buy, separately from
  energy, so someone has to forecast the price spread, and forecast errors
  burn real money. <strong>Implicit</strong>: capacity is an input to the
  energy auction itself: nobody buys it, the algorithm allocates it while
  clearing the market. Everything in sections 3–5 is Europe applying this
  lesson to one timeframe after another.
</Callout>

## 3. 🔗 Day-ahead: from three regional experiments to one algorithm

Before the history, it helps to be precise about what is being coupled.

### What a day-ahead auction actually is

A **power exchange** is a trading platform where buyers and sellers of
electricity meet anonymously; the exchange sits in the middle of every trade,
so nobody has to worry about whether the counterparty will pay. Its main
product is the **day-ahead auction**: one auction per day, held around noon,
that sets a price for every delivery period of the *next* day.

The daily rhythm (from the book's annex) is strict:

1. By **11:00** Central European Time, the TSOs publish how much cross-border
   capacity is available on each border for tomorrow.
2. Until **12:00**, market parties submit their orders to the exchange in
   their country. A sell order says "I offer this many MWh in hour 19 at
   €X/MWh or more"; a buy order says "I want this many MWh at €Y/MWh or
   less". The collected orders form the **order book**: the complete list of
   everything anyone is willing to buy or sell, at what price.
3. The order books and the capacities go to the coupling algorithm.
4. Around **12:55**, the results come back: one price per zone per period,
   plus the flows scheduled on every border.

How is the price set? By **marginal pricing**: sort the sell orders from
cheapest to most expensive, accept them until demand is covered, and the
price of the *last accepted* (marginal) order becomes the price that
*everyone* pays and receives. A tiny example, one country and one hour:

- Sell orders: 100 MWh at €20 (wind), 60 MWh at €45 (gas plant A), 80 MWh at
  €70 (gas plant B).
- Buyers need 140 MWh.
- The wind is accepted in full, gas plant A covers the remaining 40 MWh, gas
  plant B is out. The marginal order is gas plant A, so the clearing price is
  **€45** for all 140 MWh, including the wind that offered at €20.

This matters for coupling because it explains what the algorithm can do that
a trader cannot: when two countries' auctions are cleared *together*, the
algorithm sees both order books and simply accepts cheap orders in one
country to serve demand in the other, up to the cable's capacity. Nobody has
to predict any price. (How exactly prices and flows come out of a coupled
clearing is the worked numerical example I'm saving for its own post.)

Market coupling wasn't designed top-down. Starting with the Nordics in the
1990s, three regional experiments competed. They differed in one thing above
all:
how much of their order books the exchanges were willing to show each other.

What each experiment actually did:

**Market splitting (Nordics).** The easy case, because one exchange, Nord
Pool, already covered Norway, Sweden, Finland and Denmark, so there was one
order book by construction. Its algorithm worked in two steps. Step one:
clear the whole region as if the cables were infinite, producing a single
**system price**. Step two: check whether the cross-border flows implied by
that clearing actually fit on the cables. If they don't (say, cheap
hydropower in the north exceeds what the cables south can carry), *split* the
region into smaller price areas: the exporting area gets a lower price, the
importing area a higher one, and the cable between them is used exactly to
its limit. Hence the name.

<ExperimentDiagram kind="splitting" />

**Trilateral market coupling (Belgium, France, the Netherlands).** The hard
case: three separate exchanges (APX, Belpex, Powernext) and three TSOs
(TenneT, Elia, RTE) wanted the same result *without* merging into one
exchange. Their first idea was to share only a summary instead of the raw
bids: each exchange would send a **net export curve**, one line per hour
saying "at a price of X, my country would in total export (or import) this
many MW". A central algorithm would intersect these curves, fix the
cross-border volumes, and each exchange would then compute its own national
prices with those volumes locked in. Elegant, and Meeus worked on it himself,
but his research also showed where it breaks: the summary curve throws away
information about orders that link hours together (see block orders below),
so the volumes the central step fixes can be wrong for the actual order
books. After a piloting phase the partners accepted this and went live in
2006 with the honest version: the exchanges share their full order books, and
one algorithm clears everything. They take turns running it, or run it in
parallel, partly to build resilience into the procedure.

<ExperimentDiagram kind="trilateral" />

**Volume coupling (Germany and the Nordics).** Two years after the trilateral
project dropped the net-export-curve idea, EEX and Nord Pool revived it under
a new name: a central algorithm decides only the *volumes* flowing between
the regions, and each exchange prices its own market afterwards. The problems
Meeus's research had predicted for the trilateral case duly appeared (his
follow-up paper is literally titled "Implicit auctioning on the Kontek Cable:
third time lucky?"; it wasn't). After several repair attempts the project was
stopped. This short-lived experiment settled the competition between designs
for good: full order-book sharing won.

### Who runs what today

The trilateral design scaled up region by region, and the CACM guideline
(2015) made it legally binding everywhere: the **single day-ahead coupling
(SDAC)**. At the book's writing it covered 22 countries and roughly 90% of
European electricity consumption, and it has kept growing since. Three
acronyms describe the machinery:

- **NEMO** (Nominated Electricity Market Operator): a power exchange that a
  country has officially designated to run day-ahead and intraday coupling.
  The CACM default is *competing* NEMOs in each country, and a NEMO certified
  in one country may offer its services in the others; but a country that had
  a legal exchange monopoly when CACM entered into force could keep it, and
  Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Ireland, Hungary, Bulgaria, Slovakia, the
  Czech Republic and Romania did.
- **MCO function** (Market Coupling Operator): not a company but a *task*
  defined in CACM: matching the orders from all bidding zones and allocating
  the cross-border capacity in one calculation. The NEMOs must operate it
  jointly. In practice they rotate daily: one NEMO acts as **operator** and
  runs the system, one as **coordinator** (announces official results, calls
  the incident committee if something breaks), one stands by as **hot
  backup**, and the rest may recompute everything in parallel to validate the
  results. Competitors forced to run one shared machine together.
- **EUPHEMIA**: the algorithm itself (Pan-European Hybrid Electricity Market
  Integration Algorithm; the "hybrid" is real, as we'll see). It grew out of
  the trilateral project's algorithm and has roughly **ten minutes** each day
  to clear the entire European day-ahead market.

### Open issue 1: governance, or competitors running a shared monopoly

The NEMOs must operate the MCO function together while competing for the same
traders. The book traces where this tension has already led:

- During the drafting of CACM, regulators asked whether exchanges should now
  be *regulated as monopolies*, since together they hold a monopoly on
  cross-border trade in the day-ahead timeframe. Meeus's own research
  concluded the competitive model is worth keeping (services like trader
  interfaces, fees and settlement genuinely benefit from competition), but
  warned that market coupling must not turn into a *cartel* of exchanges.
- The warning aged well: in 2014 the European Commission fined EPEX Spot and
  Nord Pool Spot, the two dominant exchanges, **€5.9 million** for agreeing
  not to compete with each other and dividing European territories between
  them.
- ENTSO-E (the TSOs) has since argued for separating the MCO function more
  strictly from the NEMOs' commercial business, preferably with TSO
  involvement; ACER floated the more radical option of a **single independent
  MCO entity**, which CACM allows as a fallback. The Commission looked at it
  in 2018 and decided it was too early to act. The question is still open.

### Open issue 2: who pays for the machine

The costs of building and running the coupling systems are borne by the
NEMOs. CACM says TSOs *may* chip in but are not obliged to, and where they
don't, NEMOs may recover the remaining costs through regulated fees. The
catch: those cost-sharing arrangements are decided nationally, while several
NEMOs operate across many countries. The same exchange can find its coupling
costs covered in one country and left on its own books in another, and the
difference ends up in its trading fees.

### Open issue 3: the algorithm is being pushed to its limits

The single-market approach stands on one algorithm, so its limits are
everyone's limits. Three angles:

**When it fails, everyone notices.** On 7 June 2019 a software bug at EPEX
Spot (the biggest exchange in central Europe) meant its order books could not
be fed into EUPHEMIA. The fallback was brutal in its simplicity: the affected
countries cleared as isolated national markets with no cross-border trade
that day. Prices came out unusually low in some countries and extremely high
in others, which is the coupling benefit made visible by removing it for 24
hours.

**It carries two market cultures at once.** The "hybrid" in EUPHEMIA's name
is the heart of this issue. When the NEMOs were asked under CACM to propose
which order types they would support, they simply listed everything anyone
already used, which is the union of two traditions:

- **Multi-part orders**, typical of power *pools*: a bid that describes the
  power plant behind it, with separate terms for start-up cost, minimum
  output and how fast it can ramp up or down. The algorithm decides how to
  run the plant.
- **Block orders**, typical of power *exchanges*: all-or-nothing bids
  spanning several periods, such as "sell 100 MW in every hour from 00:00 to
  06:00 at an average price of at least €40, or nothing at all". Useful for a
  plant that cannot profitably start for a single hour.

Block orders force an awkward compromise called **paradoxically rejected
blocks**. EUPHEMIA is never allowed to accept a block that would lose money
at the final prices, but it *is* allowed to reject a block that would have
been profitable. The example below shows why that is unavoidable rather than
a bug:

<Toggle title="A worked example of a paradoxically rejected block">
  A coal plant bids a block: 100 MW in all six night hours, at an average
  price of at least €40/MWh.

  Without the block, the night hours clear at an average of €42/MWh. Looking
  at those published prices, the block is in the money: €42 is above the €40
  limit, and the plant owner will be upset to see it rejected.

  But now suppose the algorithm *had* accepted it. The extra 100 MW of cheap
  supply in every night hour pushes the marginal order down, and the night
  average drops to, say, €36/MWh. That is below the block's €40 limit, so
  accepting it would have violated its own condition.

  There is no price at which this block can be accepted consistently, so the
  algorithm rejects it even though the final prices make it look profitable.
  The block was rejected *because accepting it would have destroyed the very
  prices that make it attractive*.
</Toggle>

Power pools handle the same tension differently, with **complex pricing**:
plants whose technical bids don't fit the clearing price receive **side
payments** (an administered top-up, sometimes called an uplift, recovered
from other market participants) so that nobody runs at a loss. European
exchanges deliberately do not do side payments; a block is either accepted at
the clearing price or rejected. Whether Europe should switch is a live
academic debate: colleagues of Meeus argue complex pricing would actually
*simplify* the algorithm, while Meeus's side of the argument is that the
extra trade gains are small compared to the machinery of administering side
payments. Changing it would require amending CACM.

**And the workload keeps growing.** More countries keep joining, and EUPHEMIA
still has the same ten-minute window. The book predicted the granularity
would increase, and it did: since 30 September 2025 the coupled day-ahead
market clears **15-minute periods**, which means 96 prices per zone per day
instead of 24, in the same ten minutes.

One thing that did get settled: harmonised price limits. CACM required the
NEMOs to propose common minimum and maximum prices, the national regulators
could not reach a unanimous agreement, and the decision fell to ACER (this
escalation path is itself a CACM design feature). ACER set the day-ahead cap
at **€3,000/MWh** and the floor at **−€500/MWh**, and made the cap dynamic:
if the clearing price ever exceeds 60% of the cap, the cap rises by €1,000.
A ceiling that steps out of the way cannot act as a hidden price cap.

<Callout icon="⚡" title="Yes, negative prices are real">
  The floor is negative because prices below zero are meaningful: a nuclear
  plant that would rather pay €20/MWh for a few hours than go through a
  shutdown-restart cycle, or a subsidised wind farm that earns support per
  MWh produced, will happily bid negative. A price floor of −€500 leaves room
  for that.
</Callout>

## 4. 📅 Before day-ahead: the one place explicit auctions survive

Everything months or years ahead of delivery is the **forward market**, and
it works differently: mostly bilateral deals and over-the-counter (OTC)
trading, with exchanges offering standardised futures as trading platforms
rather than daily auctions. No daily auction means there is nothing to couple,
so for this timeframe cross-border capacity is *still* sold in explicit
auctions, the method day-ahead abandoned.

The TSOs run these auctions jointly through **JAO** (the Joint Allocation
Office in Luxembourg), which started as a voluntary initiative and became the
single European allocation platform under the FCA guideline (2016). TSOs must
offer capacity at least year-ahead and month-ahead; how much to release at
each horizon is still argued about: traders want lots of capacity early (it's
their hedge), TSOs prefer drip-feeding it (early promises can bite them
later).

Why would anyone buy these rights? Not to speculate, mostly to **hedge**.
A Dutch industrial buying German power for next year is exposed to the
DE–NL price spread; a long-term transmission right locks that spread in.

The interesting design question is what kind of right you get, and this is
where two acronyms earn their keep. A **PTR** (physical transmission right)
lets you actually nominate a flow across the border. But under the
**use-it-or-sell-it** principle (UIOSI), if you don't nominate, the capacity
is handed to the day-ahead coupling and you're paid the day-ahead spread,
so you lose nothing by doing nothing. An **FTR** (financial transmission right)
drops the physical part entirely: it's a pure payout on the spread. Play with
the slider and watch what happens:

Two more details worth remembering from this section:

- **Curtailment.** If a TSO has to cancel rights it sold (a network problem),
  compensation depends on the cause: *force majeure* refunds the auction
  price, ordinary curtailment pays the full day-ahead spread, the holder's
  lost opportunity. Who judges what counts as force majeure? The TSO's
  national regulator, which the book flags as an unresolved asymmetry.
- **You don't need transmission rights at all** if other hedges exist. The
  Nordics issue no long-term rights on internal borders; traders hedge
  location risk with **EPAD** contracts (which pay the difference between a
  zone's price and the Nordic system price). Italy does something similar
  around its national reference price (PUN).

## 5. ⏱️ After day-ahead: intraday, the youngest and least finished market

Between the day-ahead auction and real time sits the **intraday market**,
where positions get corrected as reality diverges from plans: a wind
forecast shifts, a plant trips. It matters far beyond its (small) traded
volumes, because it's the last chance to fix an imbalance in the market
before the TSO fixes it for you at balancing prices.

Intraday trading in Europe is **continuous**: a shared order book where bids
match the moment they cross, like a stock exchange, rather than in one big
auction. Cross-border continuous trading went live on 12 June 2018 (the XBID
platform, formally **SIDC**, short for single intraday coupling). The CACM guideline
requires cross-border intraday trading to stay open until at most one hour
before delivery; within a country you can often trade even closer to real
time.

The awkward part, which the book calls out directly: in continuous trading,
cross-border capacity is used **free of charge, first-come-first-served**:
whoever's order matches first gets the cable. That is *not* a market-based
allocation, the very principle Regulation 1228/2003 established. When the
capacity is scarce and valuable (say, before a windy evening), giving it away
by speed rewards the fastest algorithms rather than the highest-value trades.
The regulators' answer, reached after they failed to agree and ACER had to
decide in 2019, was to bolt **three pan-European intraday auctions** (IDAs) onto the
continuous market, so scarce capacity gets priced at least three times per
day. The book left this as an open question; the auctions actually launched
on 13 June 2024, clearing at 15:00 and 22:00 the day before delivery and at
10:00 on the delivery day.

<Callout icon="🎯" title="Why this section matters for forecasting work">
  The book notes that intraday markets are "especially important for new
  players and renewable energy technologies, which would otherwise be exposed
  to high balancing costs". That is exactly the value chain a better forecast
  feeds: day-ahead you trade the forecast, intraday you trade the forecast
  <em>updates</em>, and whatever error survives gate closure gets settled at
  balancing prices. The tighter the intraday market, the more a forecast
  improvement is worth before real time.
</Callout>

## The five answers in one paragraph

Historical privileges were dismantled by the courts (VEMW, 2005). Market-based
allocation became mandatory in 2003, and Europe learned the hard way that
*explicit* auctions (selling the cable separately) waste real money.
Day-ahead converged on *implicit* allocation: market coupling, grown from
regional experiments into SDAC, one algorithm clearing nearly all of Europe.
Before day-ahead, explicit auctions survive as long-term transmission rights
on the JAO platform, drifting from physical to financial form. After
day-ahead, intraday trading is continuous and its capacity was long given
away for free; the newly added intraday auctions are the still-unfinished
fix.

<Toggle title="Acronym cheat sheet for this chapter">
  **CACM GL**: Capacity Allocation and Congestion Management guideline
  (2015): the EU regulation that makes day-ahead and intraday coupling
  binding.

  **FCA GL**: Forward Capacity Allocation guideline (2016): same, for
  long-term transmission rights.

  **SDAC / SIDC**: Single Day-Ahead Coupling / Single Intraday Coupling: the
  two operational pan-European coupling systems.

  **NEMO**: Nominated Electricity Market Operator: a certified power
  exchange participating in the coupling.

  **MCO**: Market Coupling Operator function: the jointly-run task of
  computing all prices and cross-border flows.

  **EUPHEMIA**: the day-ahead clearing algorithm.

  **XBID**: the shared IT platform behind continuous intraday coupling.

  **IDA**: the three pan-European intraday auctions (live since June 2024).

  **JAO**: Joint Allocation Office: the single platform auctioning long-term

  **PTR / FTR**: physical / financial transmission right.

  **UIOSI**: use-it-or-sell-it: unused physical rights are resold in the

  **OTC**: over-the-counter: bilateral trades struck outside an exchange,

  **EPAD**: the Nordic contract for hedging the gap between a zone price and
  the system price, used instead of transmission rights.
