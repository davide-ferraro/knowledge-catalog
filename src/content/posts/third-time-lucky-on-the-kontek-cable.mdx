In the [[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|chapter 2 notes]],
volume coupling got two paragraphs: the failed experiment whose problems
"Meeus's research had predicted", with a paper title used as a punchline. This
post is the full story behind that punchline. The paper is Leonardo Meeus
(2011), "Implicit auctioning on the Kontek Cable: Third time lucky?",
published in *Energy Economics*; a free working-paper version exists (EUI
RSCAS 2010/49), and everything below comes from it unless linked otherwise.

Why this paper is worth a post of its own: it is a rare natural experiment.
One and the same cable tried **three different designs for implicit
auctioning** in sequence, under comparable conditions, with public hourly
data. That makes it possible to do something normally impossible in market
design: measure the designs against each other and put a euro number on each
idea.

## The setting

The **Kontek cable** is a 550 MW connection (in both directions) between East
Denmark and Germany, in operation since 1995. On the Danish side the power
exchange is Nord Pool, on the German side EEX. For years the cable barely had
a market: most of its capacity was reserved by old **priority access
contracts**, the historical privileges from section 1 of the chapter 2 notes;
the last of them expired in 2006. Explicit auctions for the remaining
capacity only started in 2002.

Then came three attempts to do better:

## Attempt 1 (2005): one exchange on both sides

Nord Pool's idea was simple: if the problem is coordinating two exchanges,
become both of them. It opened its own trading platform in Germany, so its
auction could see bids on both sides of the cable and allocate the capacity
internally, like Nordic market splitting extended across the border.

The catch: EEX did not go away. Most German volume kept trading on EEX, so
Germany now had **two prices**, one quoted by Nord Pool's thin German
platform and one by EEX. Someone still had to arbitrage between those two,
with the same guesswork as under explicit auctions. A study by Kristiansen
(2007) found the two German prices differed significantly, and that Nord
Pool's German price tracked East Denmark more closely than it tracked EEX.
The paper's verdict: this design sits *in between* explicit and implicit
auctioning. Better than nothing, but the cross-border arbitrage was still
being done by traders' forecasts rather than by an algorithm.

## Attempt 2 (2008): volume coupling, ten days

For the second attempt, Nord Pool closed its German platform and the two
exchanges founded a joint venture, the **European Market Coupling Company
(EMCC)**. The procedure, step by step:

1. EEX and Nord Pool send their order books to EMCC.
2. EMCC computes the optimal use of the cable (the volumes).
3. Each exchange inserts the result into its own order book as a
   price-taking order: the importing side gets an extra supply order, the
   exporting side an extra demand order.
4. Each exchange calculates its own price, independently.

This is what the chapter calls volume coupling (the paper also uses "dome
coupling"). Stakeholders promoted it as institutionally easier than full
price coupling: nobody has to give up their pricing.

It went live on 29 September 2008 and was suspended on 7 October 2008, after
**ten days**. The exchanges then fell back to attempt 1 for thirteen months
while they figured out what had gone wrong. The paper dissects four causes:

**1. The design flaw: prices are not unique.** This is the deep one. In an
auction with discrete orders, the optimal volumes usually do not pin down a
single price; they pin down a *range* of valid prices. A tiny example: if the
last accepted sell order in a market is at €40 and the first rejected one is
at €45, any price between €40 and €45 clears that market correctly. Now let
two exchanges each pick a price from their own range by their own rule, say
"take the lowest valid price". Germany's range might be €40 to €45 (it picks
€40) and East Denmark's €38 to €44 (it picks €38). The published result:
Denmark imports at €38 while Germany exports at €40, power apparently flowing
towards the *cheaper* market, even though the volumes were optimal and a
common price (anything from €40 to €44) existed. Nobody was in charge of
picking it. That is why "no price coordination" is not a harmless
simplification but a structural defect.

**2. The dead band.** EMCC only used the cable to the extent that the
resulting congestion rent covered the cable's grid losses. A deliberate
constraint, but one more source of unused profitable capacity.

**3. Price caps that did not match.** At the time, EEX prices could range
from −€3,000 to +€3,000/MWh while Nord Pool's could only range from €0 to
+€2,000. During the ten days of operation, the EEX price actually went beyond
Nord Pool's tighter caps in three consecutive hours: hours where the two
markets were not even *allowed* to agree on a price. (There were also hours
with negative prices on EEX while Nord Pool sat at €59.9 with no exchange
scheduled, which the paper notes cannot be explained by the caps alone.)

**4. Plain engineering.** The algorithms run by Nord Pool, EEX and EMCC did
not calculate with the same numerical precision, causing rounding
mismatches; and EEX's order book is in euros while Nord Pool's is partly in
Danish, Norwegian and Swedish crowns, and EMCC did not apply the same
exchange rate as Nord Pool when converting.

<Callout icon="🔑" title="The lesson in one sentence">
  Fixing the volumes is not enough: with discrete bids the volumes leave a
  range of valid prices, and two exchanges choosing independently from their
  own ranges will publish spreads and wrong-way flows that a single
  coordinated price would never produce. Allocation and pricing are one
  problem, not two.
</Callout>

## Attempt 3 (2009): quietly giving up on the pure idea

The relaunch on 9 November 2009 was originally meant to fix only the
engineering (the algorithm). Testing showed that was not enough, so the
coordination itself was changed: EMCC now gives Nord Pool an **indication of
what EEX's price will be**, and Nord Pool takes it into account when picking
its own price from the valid range. EEX keeps pricing independently.

That is one-way price coordination, and the paper walks through why one-way
is still imperfect: coordination works when the informed exchange happens to
be the one whose range needs adjusting, and does nothing in the mirror-image
case. The dead band also stayed, and the caps were only partly harmonised
(Nord Pool's floor moved from €0 to −€200, against EEX's −€3,000). But it
worked far better, as the numbers show.

## The scoreboard

The paper computes the welfare loss of each design from public hourly data:
for every hour, the price spread multiplied by the unused capacity (with
wrong-way flows counting as up to 1,100 MW unused, since the flow would have
to reverse). Annualised, per unit of cable capacity:

*Welfare loss per MW of cable capacity per year, from Table 1 of the paper.
In absolute terms: roughly €10.8M, €28.4M and €0.6M per year. The average
loss per hour was €1,228, €3,246 and €67; statistical tests confirm the
three levels are significantly different.*

Two things stand out:

- **Volume coupling was not just imperfect, it was worse than the halfway
  design it replaced.** Attempt 2 lost money at almost triple the rate of
  attempt 1. For calibration, the paper compares with explicit auctions on
  the French borders in 2008, which lost between €22k and €78k per MW per
  year: attempts 1 and 2 sat in the same league as the method implicit
  auctioning was supposed to replace.
- **Coordinating prices, even one-way, cut the loss by a factor of about
  fifty.** Attempt 3 still leaked about €0.5M a year (the one-way asymmetry
  and the dead band), but it clearly outperformed everything before it.

Hence the title's answer: yes, third time lucky, but only because the third
implementation partly abandoned volume coupling. The "institutionally easier"
idea of coupling volumes while keeping pricing sovereign is exactly the part
that had to go.

## Why this paper mattered

The point was never one 550 MW cable. In 2008-2010, volume coupling was being
promoted (including in a joint paper by the European associations of power
exchanges and TSOs) as *the* model for coupling Europe's regions, precisely
because it let every exchange keep its own pricing. This paper, together with
the author's earlier work showing why price coordination between exchanges is
essential, put empirical numbers on the counterargument.

The market listened. In March 2010, while the third implementation was still
running, six exchanges (APX-Endex, Belpex, EPEX Spot, GME, Nord Pool Spot and
OMEL) launched the joint project to build one shared **price coupling**
algorithm for Europe. That project's algorithm is EUPHEMIA, the one that
clears nearly all of Europe's day-ahead market today. EMCC's volume coupling
lived on only as a temporary bridge between the Nordic and Central West
European regions until 4 February 2014, when full price coupling of
North-Western Europe went live and retired it.

If you want to *feel* the failure mode rather than read about it, the
[[who-gets-the-rights-to-trade-across-borders|chapter 2 post]] has an
interactive volume-coupling model where you fix the cable volume yourself and
watch the two independently computed prices diverge.
